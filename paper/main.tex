\begin{abstract}
Acoustic source localization through severe barriers can fail catastrophically with conventional microphone arrays due to strong attenuation, barrier-induced distributed re-radiation, and vulnerability to same-room coherent interference. To address these limitations, we propose a heterogeneous sensing framework that fuses physical microphones with a laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) probing structure-borne vibration as a reference anchor that can retain a higher effective signal-to-jammer ratio (SJR) in our testbed. Rather than feeding barrier-colored spectrograms into deep learning models, we introduce a zero-shot Physics-Informed Geometric Search (PI-GS) that computes cross-modal Generalized Cross-Correlation (GCC-PHAT) spatial features between the LDV and microphones and performs a joint geometric search. PI-GS is an engineering solver: it does not assume a universal barrier inversion model, and it is effective when the LDV--microphone pairs retain a usable LDV-coherent lag structure in the analysis band after time-frequency aggregation. In experimental evaluations, microphone-only baselines are frequently captured by structural multipath and spurious peaks (including $\tau\!\approx\!0$ collapse), while PI-GS localizes the target with a mean absolute error of $1.48^\circ$ across testing sequences without requiring neural network training.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Direction-of-Arrival (DoA) estimation is a cornerstone of intelligent acoustic systems, enabling applications such as hearing aids, robotic navigation, and search-and-rescue \cite{brandstein2001microphone, cobos2017survey}. Conventional generalized cross-correlation with phase transform (GCC-PHAT) \cite{knapp1976gcc}, sub-space methods like SRP-PHAT \cite{dibiase2001robust, shmuel2023deep}, and sophisticated deep neural networks (DNNs) \cite{grumiaux2022dnn, sun2024msdet} reliably locate sources in free-field or moderately reverberant environments using arrays of microphones. However, these homogeneous acoustic sensing paradigms break down in strongly attenuating environments, such as localizing a survivor screaming behind concrete debris or tracking a source through thick structural barriers \cite{remillieux2009ldv, wang2024physics}. 

The failure mode in through-barrier settings is practical and repeatable: attenuation and distributed re-radiation can severely reduce the usable spatial coherence of microphone arrays and distort TDoA cues. The barrier acts as a complex, material-dependent filter, distorting the spectral envelope \cite{li2024physics}. If a jammer operates in the receiving room, standard TDoA features can become dominated by the unobstructed jammer \cite{adavanne2019seld, kulkarni2024doa}. In this paper we focus on a single dominant speech source behind a barrier under strong receiving-room interference. To address this, we explore an actively augmented sensing modality: the Laser Doppler Vibrometer (LDV). An LDV remotely measures surface velocity by detecting the Doppler shift of a reflected laser beam \cite{rothberg2017ldv}, capturing structure-borne vibration on the wall \cite{reed2006ldvspeech, bocko2023estimating}. In practice, this vibration reference can be less sensitive to in-room airborne interference than microphone pressure, providing a useful anchor under low signal-to-jammer ratio (SJR) conditions.

In this paper, we propose a heterogeneous LDV-Microphone fusion framework for robust through-barrier DoA estimation. Rather than learning a barrier-specific mapping from raw, barrier-colored spectrograms, our primary contribution is a zero-shot Physics-Informed Geometric Search (PI-GS) algorithm. PI-GS computes cross-modal GCC-PHAT features between the LDV anchor and physical microphones and enforces joint geometric consistency, reducing sensitivity to magnitude coloration without training data. Through physical hardware evaluation against an adversarial near-field barrier, we show that PI-GS achieves a mean absolute error of $1.48^\circ$, while microphone-only baselines are strongly degraded by structural multipath. Furthermore, we demonstrate improved robustness under coherent jammers in the receiving room in our testbed.

\subsection{Microphone Vulnerability and Structural Error}
\label{sec:mic_vuln}

To locate a target using TDoA, standard algorithms rely on tracking time delays. In conventional free-space estimation, a point source $s(t)$ arrives at a microphone $m$ with a simple geometric delay $\tau_m$:
\begin{equation}
  x_{m}(t) = s(t - \tau_m) + n_m(t). \label{eq:free_space}
\end{equation}
When an obstacle is introduced, algorithms commonly mis-model it as a simple scalar transfer function $h_{\text{wall}}(t)$ that merely colors the spectrum without destroying the spatial geometry:
\begin{equation}
  x_{m}(t) = \left[ s(t - \tau_m) * h_{\text{wall}}(t) \right] + n_m(t). \label{eq:naive_wall}
\end{equation}
If Eq.~\ref{eq:naive_wall} held true, standard cross-correlation could still extract $\tau_m$. However, acoustic propagation through a barrier is often a two-stage process: the source wave excites the structure, and the excited surface then re-radiates sound into the room. Unlike a far-field point source, the near-field barrier behaves as a distributed secondary radiator. Let $v(\vec{r}_w, t)$ be the structural velocity at point $\vec{r}_w$ on the barrier surface $S$. The resulting near-field acoustic pressure received by a microphone at $\vec{r}_m$ can be expressed via the Rayleigh surface integral:
%
\begin{equation}
  x_{m}(t) = a_m^{s} \iint_S v\left(\vec{r}_w, t - \frac{|\vec{r}_w - \vec{r}_m|}{c}\right) \frac{d\vec{r}_w}{|\vec{r}_w - \vec{r}_m|} + i_m(t), \label{eq:mics}
\end{equation}
%
where $a_m^s$ encapsulates the acoustic radiation scaling, and $i_m(t)$ represents the local in-room jammer reverberation and sensor noise. This spatial integration can inject frequency-dependent phase distortions into the inter-channel TDoA, making mic-only localization unreliable even without a jammer, and worse under strong in-room interference.

\subsection{LDV Anchor Isolation}
\label{sec:ldv_isol}

Conversely, the LDV queries the structure-borne vibration at a singular, discrete surface point $\vec{r}_{\text{ldv}} \in S$:
%
\begin{equation}
  x_{\text{ldv}}(t)  = v(\vec{r}_{\text{ldv}}, t) + n_{\text{ldv}}(t)
  \label{eq:ldv}
\end{equation}
%
Contrasting this point measurement against the 2D surface integration of Eq.~\ref{eq:mics} highlights a key advantage of the LDV: it measures a localized wall response rather than a surface-integrated acoustic field. Moreover, due to air--structure impedance mismatch and the wall's mechanical dynamics, in-room airborne jammer waves often couple less efficiently into the measured wall vibration than into microphone pressure. As a result, the LDV can provide a cleaner target reference in low-SJR conditions in our setup, although we do not assume perfect isolation.

The dual combination of spatial phase blurring and spectral distortion poses a critical challenge. Feeding raw audio into generic deep learning models can encourage memorizing barrier-specific acoustic colorations and geometry \cite{ueno2023physics, olivieri2024physics}. To improve zero-shot robustness, our framework avoids black-box training and uses a purely analytical geometric solver (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\textit{Scope and assumptions:} We target a single dominant source behind a barrier and use the LDV as a reference anchor that can have a higher effective SJR in our setup. PI-GS does not claim universal barrier inversion; instead, it is an engineering method that succeeds when the LDV--microphone pairs retain a usable LDV-coherent lag structure in the analysis band after time-frequency aggregation. Let $X_v(f)$ denote the STFT of $x_{\text{ldv}}(t)$ and $X_m(f)$ the STFT of microphone $m$. PI-GS computes $R_{VL}(\tau)$ and $R_{VR}(\tau)$ and scores each candidate $\mathbf{p}$ by evaluating these functions at predicted relative lags.

\subsection{Cross-Modal GCC Features}
\label{sec:gcc_features}

By discarding magnitude information, GCC-PHAT reduces sensitivity to magnitude coloration and emphasizes phase-consistent structure \cite{yamada2023speech}. We compute the Generalized Cross-Correlation with Phase Transform (GCC-PHAT) \cite{knapp1976gcc} between the LDV STFT $X_v(f)$ and each microphone STFT $X_m(f)$:
%
\begin{equation}
  R_{Vm}(\tau) = \int \frac{X_v(f)\,X_m^*(f)}{\left|X_v(f)\,X_m^*(f)\right|}\,e^{j2\pi f\tau}\,df, \quad m \in \{L, R\}
  \label{eq:gcc_vm}
\end{equation}
%
To connect Eq.~\ref{eq:gcc_vm} to the heterogeneous sensing physics, we separate the microphone observation into an LDV-coherent component (originating from a neighborhood of $\vec{r}_{\text{ldv}}$) and a residual term that aggregates the remaining wall surface and in-room interference:
%
\begin{equation}
  X_m(f) = U_{\text{ldv}}(f)\,B_m(f)\,e^{-j 2\pi f |\vec{r}_{\text{ldv}} - \vec{r}_m|/c} + X_{m,\text{rest}}(f),
  \label{eq:cross}
\end{equation}
%
where $U_{\text{ldv}}(f)$ is the (unknown) wall response at $\vec{r}_{\text{ldv}}$ driven by the source and $X_{m,\text{rest}}(f)$ aggregates the dense ``Coherence Trap'' contributions from $S \setminus \vec{r}_{\text{ldv}}$ and jammer terms. The factor $B_m(f)$ collects radiation/geometry effects and is treated as slowly varying over the analysis band. Under Eq.~\ref{eq:cross}, PHAT-GCC does not \emph{guarantee} barrier removal; it provides a useful spatial feature when the LDV-coherent component is non-negligible relative to $X_{m,\text{rest}}$ (e.g., after time-frequency aggregation). In our implementation we search over the full lag range and use a magnitude-based criterion (e.g., $|R_{Vm}(\tau)|$) to reduce sensitivity to residual phase rotations.

To overcome this corrupted landscape, our algorithm acts as a \textit{Physics-Informed Geometric Search} (PI-GS), leveraging geometric consistency between $\tau_{VL}(\mathbf{p})$ and $\tau_{VR}(\mathbf{p})$ as a constraint.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Geometric Joint Search}
\label{sec:joint_search}

PI-GS uses the LDV as a shared anchor and combines two independent cross-modal features (LDV--MicL and LDV--MicR) with a geometric consistency constraint.

In a near-field scenario, defining the delay purely by target angle ($\theta$) implicitly assumes a known fixed distance. PI-GS instead searches a 2D source coordinate $\mathbf{p} = (X, Y)$ within a physical manifold $\Omega$, where $X$ is parallel to the barrier and $Y$ is the range (normal) direction. For any hypothesized $\mathbf{p}$, we compute a free-space travel-time template to the LDV point and to each microphone, $\tau_V(\mathbf{p})$ and $\tau_m(\mathbf{p})$, using Euclidean distances and sound speed $c$, and form the relative cross-modal delay template $\tau_{Vm}(\mathbf{p}) = \tau_m(\mathbf{p}) - \tau_V(\mathbf{p})$ for $m \in \{L, R\}$. We denote these as $\tau_{VL}(\mathbf{p})$ and $\tau_{VR}(\mathbf{p})$ for notational clarity. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:mics} implies the microphone observation is a distributed re-radiation field, this template provides a practical geometric constraint: PI-GS succeeds when the LDV-coherent component yields a dominant lag that varies consistently with $\mathbf{p}$ across both microphones.

\textit{Evaluation convention (testbed):} In our cardboard setup, the ground-truth source locations are specified by a lateral coordinate $X=x_s$ along the barrier, with a fixed range in our evaluation. We therefore visualize and evaluate 1D slices of the objective along $X$ (with $Y$ fixed). For reporting DoA in degrees, we use the standard mic-pair convention with spacing $d$ ($d=1.4$\,m in our setup): after obtaining $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$, we compute the implied mic-pair delay $\tau_{LR}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})=\tau_L(\hat{\mathbf{p}})-\tau_R(\hat{\mathbf{p}})$ and map it to an equivalent azimuth angle $\hat{\theta}=\arcsin(c\,\tau_{LR}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})/d)$.

The PI-GS core operation establishes a unified joint scoring objective across the LDV anchor. It extracts the spatially-whitened correlation magnitude of both the left and right sensor pairs at their predicted offsets:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{p} \in \Omega} \left[ \left|R_{VL}\big(\tau_{VL}(\mathbf{p})\big)\right| + \left|R_{VR}\big(\tau_{VR}(\mathbf{p})\big)\right| \right]
  \label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
%
This joint objective acts as a form of spatial focusing. While barrier-induced multipath (Eq.~\ref{eq:cross}) can inject many secondary peaks into $R_{VL}(\tau)$ and $R_{VR}(\tau)$ individually, many of these peaks are not jointly consistent with a single source hypothesis under the shared LDV anchor. Enforcing Eq.~\ref{eq:obj} therefore favors locations whose predicted relative lags align simultaneously across both LDV-microphone pairs, reducing susceptibility to spurious peaks when a usable LDV-coherent component exists in both channels.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

To validate the proposed heterogeneous system under severe attenuation, we construct a physical testbed mimicking a search-and-rescue or surveillance scenario.

\subsection{Acoustic Environment and Hardware}
A standard cardboard partition separates the source and receiving rooms, acting as a broadband ``Coherence Trap'' that can scramble spatial phase relationships via near-field diffraction. A studio monitor plays LibriSpeech segments. To simulate worst-case near-field occlusion, the barrier is placed $25\text{\,cm}$ from the target speaker across five azimuth angles (spanning $\pm25^\circ$).

To support segment-level evaluation, we also record a separate chirp reference at the same nominal positions. Table~\ref{tab:validation} reports errors on the chirp reference slice (0--2\,s) and on a speech slice (3--8\,s) from separate recordings.

In the receiving room, two measurement microphones are placed $1.75\text{\,m}$ from the barrier with a wide $1.4\text{\,m}$ spacing, exacerbating spatial mismatch. An industrial LDV targets the barrier surface opposite the speaker's axis. 

To evaluate interference robustness, a "jammer" speaker broadcasts continuous babble noise inside the receiving room, $1.0\text{\,m}$ from the microphones. Due to the barrier's significant transmission loss, the \emph{effective} Signal-to-Jammer Ratio (SJR) at the microphones is profoundly negative even at moderate jammer volumes.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
Signals are acquired at a hardware sample rate of 48\,kHz. To extract spatial features, we compute GCC-PHAT from STFTs with $n_{\text{fft}}=1024$ and aggregate the resulting correlation features over 0.5-second windows. PI-GS then sweeps the physical coordinate manifold $\Omega$ using the joint objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:obj} without requiring neural network pre-training.

This control suggests that the blocked-condition failures are driven by barrier-induced distributed re-radiation and the resulting multipath-dominated correlation landscape. In contrast, PI-GS reduces sensitivity to these effects by leveraging an LDV anchor and a joint geometric consistency constraint, improving stability across incidence angles in our experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a heterogeneous sensing framework that utilizes a Laser Doppler Vibrometer as a physical anchor for through-barrier DoA estimation. To combat spatial blurring induced by barrier re-radiation, we developed a Zero-Shot Physics-Informed Geometric Search (PI-GS) that processes cross-modal GCC-PHAT features, avoiding black-box training. PI-GS is an engineering solver and does not claim universal barrier inversion; it is effective when the LDV--microphone pairs retain a usable LDV-coherent lag structure in the analysis band. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that while conventional microphone arrays can fail under severe attenuation and structural multipath, PI-GS reduces sensitivity to these distortions, achieving a mean absolute error of $1.48^\circ$ and strong robustness under in-room jammers in our testbed. Future work will formalize information-theoretic lower bounds governing structure-borne vs.\ air-borne spatial coherence across diverse barrier materials.
